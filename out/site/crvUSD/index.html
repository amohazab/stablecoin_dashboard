One test asks what happens to the gap between assets and liabilities, measured here as bad debt, if the PegKeepers were switched off so they could no longer mint or burn crvUSD to defend the peg. The result shows no change at all: bad debt comes out at $6 (5.96) whether the PegKeepers are left running or switched off. That tells you this particular shortfall does not come from the PegKeepers' buying and selling activity — something else is driving it, so removing their defence mechanism here neither helps nor hurts the number.

A second test asks what happens if the price oracle is allowed to lag the market by its full moving-average window, rather than tracking price instantly. Under instant pricing the upper bound on collateral value at risk is $0, but once the lag is applied it jumps to $31.7M. The underlying coverage figures show why: at a 50 percent shock the collateral-to-debt ratio falls from 1.203998 with instant pricing to 0.790398 once the oracle lags the market, and at a 70 percent shock it falls from 1.125962 to 0.372248. In both cases the lagged figure is markedly worse, and at the higher shock level it drops well below the point where collateral would fully cover debt. This shows that the delay built into the oracle's averaging window, not the size of the shock alone, is what turns a fully covered position into a shortfall — the slower the price catches up, the more collateral value can already have been lost by the time it is recognised.

When the collateral behind this stablecoin drops sharply in price, crvUSD's LLAMMA system moves positions through soft liquidation: it converts collateral inside the crossed price bands as the price falls, rather than waiting for a single default event. PegKeepers, which can mint or burn crvUSD against paired stable pools, sit alongside this as a separate stabilizing mechanism.

Before the shock, collateral coverage across the book stood at 0.99. After the shock modeled here, coverage rose to 1.20, a gap of 0.2118 restored by the liquidation mechanism converting collateral through the bands. Bad debt in this scenario came to $6, a small figure against the book. Forced sales — the collateral that must be sold — also came to $6, measured against exit liquidity of $20.4M, giving a forced-sales-to-exit-liquidity ratio of under 0.1%.

Bad debt on this grid is structurally zero: soft liquidation converts collateral at band prices before positions become insolvent, so the mechanism itself prevents the kind of shortfall that would otherwise register as bad debt.

At higher levels of liquidity-provider flight in the exit-liquidity grid, some cells show exit liquidity falling to $0, which leaves the forced-sales-to-exit-liquidity ratio undefined at that point — there is no exit venue left to absorb any forced selling, whatever its size.

crvUSD is not directly minted or redeemed against the paired stablecoin at 0xdac17f…1ec7, so a depeg of that paired asset does not force crvUSD's mechanism itself to sell collateral. This is why forced sales in the Member-2 headline depeg scenario (paired stablecoin at its middle target, no liquidity-provider flight) come out at $0, with a ratio of forced sales to exit liquidity of 0.

That zero is a statement about the mechanism, not about market exposure. Holders who want to exit crvUSD still have to sell into pools, and those pools thin out as the paired stablecoin's price target moves further from parity: exit liquidity at a price impact of 0.5% falls from $17.1M when the target sits at 0.97, to $16.2M at 0.93, to $15.2M at 0.88. The same pattern holds at wider price impacts, for example at 2.0% impact exit liquidity runs $19.8M at 0.97, $18.9M at 0.93, and $17.8M at 0.88.

Separately, the PegKeeper's usable headroom stands at $85.4M, with $6.6M of burn capacity, and its paired-unit holdings are concentrated almost entirely in one address at $6.4M, with the other listed addresses holding $0. None of this changes the forced-sale figure, but it shapes how much cushion exists around the exit venues that actually absorb selling pressure during a depeg of the paired stablecoin.

The admin-power table, row by row

Minting power sits with a dao governance holder at 0xb7400d…fb79. Any change takes effect only after a delay of 7 days, and no veto address is recorded for this action, so once the delay passes the change goes through unopposed. The same holder also controls the ceiling that limits new supply, again with a delay of 7 days, but here a veto address at 0x467947…1e0c is recorded, meaning that action can be blocked before it takes effect.

The oracle that feeds the price used for the system can also be changed by 0xb7400d…fb79, with the same delay of 7 days, and this action too carries a veto address at 0x467947…1e0c. Other core settings, grouped as parameters, follow the identical pattern: same holder, a delay of 7 days, and the same veto holder able to intervene. Pausing the system, by contrast, rests with 0x467947…1e0c directly and can happen with no delay at all.

Contract code itself cannot be upgraded, since the upgrade path is recorded as immutable, and there is no holder listed for freezing assets, blacklisting addresses, or seizing funds. So the only ways backing or minting can change run through the dao governance holder and its associated veto holder, and the fastest of these, pausing, carries no delay at all.
